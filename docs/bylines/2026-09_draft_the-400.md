# Byline draft — "The biggest x402 failure isn't adoption. It's the 400."

Draft for the keeper's name. HackerNoon (tags: x402, ai-agents,
api-design, payments). His voice, his edits; this is the shape.

⚑ Contains a correction to our own August field report. The August
report attributed the 400s to facilitator rejection of signed payments.
Re-reading the response bodies does not support that for most of them.
Numbers below re-derived from `research/field-run-2026-08-18/ledger.jsonl`
on 2026-09-08; re-derive again the day it posts.

---

**Title:** I made 1,707 paid requests to 1,589 x402 endpoints. The
biggest failure wasn't payment — it was the 400.

**Standfirst:** In August 2026 I walked every reachable domain on the
public x402 endpoint list with a funded wallet. 616 of 1,707 attempts
died on an HTTP 400. I originally reported those as facilitators
rejecting signed payments. That was wrong, and the real answer is more
useful.

---

Over August 18 and 19, 2026, I walked the entire walkable x402 set with
a funded wallet: **1,589 domains, 1,707 attempts, 489 successful paid
purchases, $5.7355 in the ledger.** Real USDC, real settlements, one
buyer, every request tagged with a calling-card user agent so operators
could see who had knocked.

The single largest failure was HTTP 400: **616 attempts, 36.1% of the
run.** When I first wrote this up I described those as "the payment is
signed per spec but the facilitator rejects it," and called it the
ecosystem's number one friction point.

I have now read the response bodies. That description is not supported.

I run scvd.store, so this is a correction to my own published report.

## What the 616 actually say

Sorting every 400 response body by what it complains about:

| What the door said | Count | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Missing or invalid **request inputs** | 276 | 44.8% |
| **No body at all** | 250 | 40.6% |
| Other (a hint, a usage string) | 50 | 8.1% |
| Anything mentioning payment, signature, nonce or authorization | 40 | **6.5%** |

Six and a half percent. The overwhelming majority of x402's biggest
failure mode is not a payment problem at all. It is doors refusing
requests that were malformed before payment ever entered the picture.

Real examples, verbatim from the ledger:

```json
{"error":"Missing required field: text"}
{"error":"channel query param required","price":"$0.002",
 "example_query":{"channel":"job-99"}}
{"error":"provide two colors: ?fg=#111111&bg=#ffffff (hex or rgb())"}
{"error":"invalid_request_body","message":"Field \"url\" is required.",
 "charged":false,"payment_accepted":false}
```

Every one of those doors is behaving reasonably. It needs a parameter.
It didn't get one. It said so.

## The gap that produces them

Here is the part that makes this a protocol story rather than a
"crawler sent empty requests" story, and I want to be straight about my
own exposure in it: **my walker sent no request body.** It read the 402
challenge, signed against the terms, and retried. That is exactly what
an x402 client is specified to do.

And that is the gap. **The x402 challenge has nowhere to put required
inputs.** It carries scheme, network, asset, amount, payTo, timeout,
resource. There is no field that says "and this endpoint needs a
`channel` query parameter." A buyer that speaks x402 fluently and
nothing else has read everything the protocol offers and still cannot
construct a valid request.

The information usually exists. Look at that third example again — the
door helpfully names its catalog. Others point at `/openapi.json`. But
it lives out of band, in a document the payment flow never references,
in a format that varies per operator.

So the sequence is: agent discovers endpoint, agent reads challenge,
agent signs, agent pays, agent gets 400. The money moved. The door has
no idea it just failed a customer who did everything the spec asked.

Two supporting numbers from the same walk. Of 1,053 endpoints that
returned a 402 to an unpaid probe, only **345 — 32.8% — carried a
parseable payment-requirements structure.** And 250 of the 616 four
hundreds returned an empty body, so even the diagnosis is unavailable:
the buyer sees a bare status code and cannot tell a missing parameter
from a rejected signature.

We named this class `inputs-undeclared` in our defect vocabulary before
I understood how much of the failure surface it was. It is the single
most expensive thing on that list, and it is invisible from the seller's
side. **In your logs, a buyer who couldn't guess your parameters and a
buyer who never wanted your product look identical.**

## The rest of the taxonomy

For completeness, from the same 1,707 attempts:

| Failure | Count |
|---|---|
| 400 (see above) | 616 |
| Still 402 after paying | 188 |
| 422 unprocessable | 124 |
| No PAYMENT-REQUIRED header on a 402 | 81 |
| Wrong status on the unpaid probe (400/404/200/405) | 123 |
| 500 / 502 | 51 |
| `payTo` is an ENS name, no resolver available | 21 |

The 188 "still 402 after paying" are the ones that genuinely look like
payment failures, and at 11% they are a real problem. But they are a
third the size of the 400.

The ENS one is worth a sentence because it's so cheap to avoid: 21
endpoints published a human-readable name where a client needs bytes to
sign over. Put the address in the challenge.

## What to do about it, both sides

**If you operate an endpoint:** answer a 402 probe with a body that
names your required inputs, even though the spec has no field for it.
Put them in the `description`, put them in an `extra` object, put them
anywhere a parser can reach — and give every 400 a body that says which
field is missing. The 250 silent ones are the worst doors in the run,
not because they're broken but because they're undiagnosable. Two of the
examples above are, by contrast, close to ideal: they name the field and
show an example value.

**If you build a buyer:** treat a 400 as reject-and-move-on, not
fix-and-retry. Re-signing the same payment against the same door will
not help, and the walk's economics say your time is better spent
elsewhere — cheap endpoints ($0.01–$0.05) settled at about 34%, and
above $0.05 success collapsed to near nothing. Don't trust `accepts[0]`;
you will meet at least four different challenge structures, and only
about a third use the standard field. And before you pay a door for the
first time, check whether it publishes an OpenAPI document or a catalog,
because that is where its required inputs are hiding.

**If you are working on the spec:** a declared-inputs field in the
challenge would remove more real-world failure than any other single
change I saw in 1,707 attempts.

## What this doesn't prove

One buyer, one wallet, two days in August, one facilitator path, and a
walker that deliberately sent no request body. A client that fetched
each endpoint's OpenAPI document first would have a completely different
failure profile — that is the point of the piece, but it is also its
limit, and I have not run that experiment.

The categorisation above is a keyword pass over stored response bodies
capped at 200 characters, not a hand-read of all 616. The 250 empty
bodies are unclassifiable by construction, and it is possible some of
them are facilitator rejections, which would raise that 6.5% figure. It
could not raise it above about 47% even if every silent one were a
payment failure.

Every entry is in the public ledger with its URL, status, error and body
preview, so anyone can re-derive these buckets with different rules and
get a different answer. I would like to see that.

One last honest note on the ledger itself: it under-reports. Reconciling
against Base showed **669 on-chain transfers totalling $6.3970** against
489 paid entries and $5.7355 recorded — a 10.3% gap, concentrated at the
cheapest tier. The script logged after the response, so any settlement
whose response never arrived settled on chain and was written down as a
failure. If your agent logs its spending after the response, so does
yours.
